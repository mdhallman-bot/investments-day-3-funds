Read: From “Bogle’s Folly” to Trillions

Current Event — September 2026

Imagine someone offered you two ways to invest your money. In the first, a professional manager studies hundreds of companies and tries to choose the investments that will perform best. In the second, nobody tries to predict the winners. Instead, your money is spread across a large group of investments by following an index.

An Idea People Laughed At

On August 31, 1976, Vanguard launched what became the Vanguard 500 Index Fund. It was the first stock index fund available to individual investors and was designed to follow the S&P 500, an index of about 500 large U.S. companies.

Most funds at the time were actively managed. Professional managers researched investments and decided what to buy and sell, usually hoping to beat the market. Vanguard founder John Bogle proposed something different: stop trying to pick the winners and simply follow the S&P 500.

Investors were skeptical. Vanguard hoped to raise $150 million when the fund launched but raised only about $11.3 million. Critics even called the idea “Bogle’s Folly.” Fifty years later, the idea looks very different.

The Basket Gets Much Bigger

By 2026, index funds held about 64% of the money invested in stock funds. More than $18 trillion was invested in stock index mutual funds and ETFs, compared with about $12 trillion in actively managed stock funds.

One reason is diversification. If an investor puts all their money into one company and that company struggles, the loss can be severe. A broad fund spreads money across many investments, so one poor performer usually has a smaller effect. But diversification reduces risk; it does not eliminate risk. A diversified fund can still lose money.

Index Fund Does Not Mean “S&P 500 Fund”

An index is simply a group of investments used to measure a particular part of a market. The S&P 500 is one example, but an index can follow technology companies, small companies, international investments, or bonds. An index fund is designed to follow one of those indexes.

That also explains why ETF and index fund are not opposites. ETF describes how a fund is structured and traded. Index fund describes how its investments are selected. An ETF can follow an index, while some ETFs are actively managed. Funds can contain stocks, bonds, or other assets.

The Small Number That Can Matter a Lot

Funds usually charge an annual fee called an expense ratio. The percentage can look tiny, but the fee reduces the return an investor keeps. Index funds can often operate more cheaply because they follow an index instead of paying a team to continually search for investments that might beat the market.

In 2025, the average expense ratio for index mutual funds was about 0.05%. Suppose two investments earned 7% before fees. If one charged 0.10%, the investor would keep about 6.90% before other costs. If another charged 1.00%, the investor would keep about 6.00%. Over decades, that difference can become more important because investment returns can compound.

Simple Does Not Mean Perfect

The growth of index funds does not mean every fund with the word “index” is automatically a good choice. Consider two index funds. One follows hundreds or thousands of companies from many industries. Another follows only technology companies. Both may be index funds, but they are not equally diversified. If technology companies perform poorly, the technology fund could be hit much harder.

Investors still need to ask: What does this fund own? Which index does it follow? How diversified is it? What is its expense ratio?

Fifty Years Later

The first index fund was built on a simple idea: instead of constantly trying to find the next winning investment, investors could buy a basket that followed a market index, keep costs low, and stay invested for the long term. In 1976, many people doubted that idea. In 2026, the Vanguard 500 Index Fund alone held roughly $1.7 trillion, and index investing had become a major part of how Americans invest.

The lesson is not that index funds cannot lose money or that every index fund is a good investment. The bigger lesson is that an investor does not necessarily have to predict the next winning company to participate in the market. A fund can let an investor buy the basket instead.
